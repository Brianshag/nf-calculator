What Is Noise Figure?

Noise figure (NF) is a measure of how much a device — such as an amplifier, mixer, or LNA — degrades the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) of a signal passing through it. It is expressed in decibels (dB), where 0 dB represents a theoretically perfect, noiseless device. In practice, all real RF components add some amount of thermal noise, and the noise figure quantifies this degradation.

A low noise figure is critical in receiver front-end design, satellite communications, radio astronomy, cellular base stations, and any application where weak signals must be detected reliably. For example, a low-noise amplifier (LNA) with a noise figure of 1.5 dB will produce a much cleaner output than one rated at 4 dB, directly improving system sensitivity and range.

How the Y-Factor Method Works

The Y-Factor method is the most widely used technique for measuring noise figure with a spectrum analyzer. It works by comparing the output noise power of a device under two conditions: with a calibrated noise source switched OFF (cold state) and switched ON (hot state).

The ratio of these two power levels is called the Y-factor. Combined with the known Excess Noise Ratio (ENR) of the noise source, the Y-factor allows the noise figure to be calculated directly. This calculator implements the full procedure described in the Rohde & Schwarz application note 1MA178, including a second-stage correction that removes the spectrum analyzer's own noise contribution from the result using the Friis cascade equation.

ℹ The second-stage correction is performed automatically using the calibration measurements (noise source only, no DUT). No additional input is required from the user.

What You Need

  • A spectrum analyzer with a marker function and averaging or max-hold capability
  • A calibrated noise source with a known ENR value (from its calibration sheet)
  • The device under test (DUT) — typically an amplifier or LNA
  • RF coaxial cables and adapters appropriate for your frequency range
  • A DC bias supply for the noise source (usually +28 V)

Step-by-Step Measurement Procedure

  1. Connect the noise source directly to the spectrum analyzer input (no DUT). Set the sweep time to 10 seconds to capture both the ON and OFF states of the noise source in a single trace.
  2. Toggle the noise source OFF and place Marker 1 on the average noise floor. Record this value as Nsrc,off in dBm.
  3. Toggle the noise source ON and place Marker 2 on the elevated noise floor. Record this value as Nsrc,on in dBm. These two readings form the calibration measurement.
  4. Insert the DUT between the noise source and the spectrum analyzer input. Keep all other settings identical.
  5. Toggle the noise source OFF and record the new noise floor as Ndut,off in dBm.
  6. Toggle the noise source ON and record the elevated noise floor as Ndut,on in dBm.
  7. Enter all four power readings and the ENR value from your noise source calibration sheet into the calculator above and click Calculate.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is ENR and where do I find it?

Excess Noise Ratio (ENR) describes how much more noise power a noise source produces when switched on compared with thermal noise at the reference temperature. It is printed on the noise source and listed in its calibration sheet, usually per frequency, so use the value closest to your measurement frequency.

Read more: What is Excess Noise Ratio (ENR)? — everything RF

Why is the Y-factor method used, and why must Y be greater than 1?

The Y-factor is the ratio of noise power with the source ON to noise power with it OFF. Switching a noise source on can only add noise, so a valid measurement always gives Y > 1. If it doesn't, the noise source is not reaching the analyzer, or the DUT or analyzer noise is swamping the source.

Read more: Noise Figure Measurement Accuracy: The Y-Factor Method — Keysight

How do I measure noise figure with a spectrum analyzer?

Connect a noise source, measure the analyzer's noise with the source ON and OFF, repeat with the DUT in place, then enter the four readings and the ENR. This calculator then applies the corrections for you.

Read more: Understanding noise figure — Rohde & Schwarz · The Y-Factor Technique for Noise Figure Measurements (1MA178)

Why does the spectrum analyzer need a second-stage correction?

A spectrum analyzer has a noise figure of its own, often 10 dB or more. When it follows the DUT, it adds noise to the measurement. The Friis cascade formula lets you subtract that contribution, divided by the DUT gain, so the result reflects the DUT alone.

Read more: Noise figure (including cascaded stages) — Wikipedia

Why is 290 K used as the reference temperature?

The IEEE standard defines noise factor relative to a source at T0 = 290 K, so noise figures from different vendors and labs can be compared directly.

Read more: Noise figure — Wikipedia

What is a good noise figure for an LNA?

It depends on frequency and technology, but around 1 dB is generally considered a good, low noise figure, and about 3 dB is typical for general-purpose LNAs. Satellite and radio astronomy front-ends go well below that.
