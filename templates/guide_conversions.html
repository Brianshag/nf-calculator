Datasheets quote noise in different ways: noise figure in dB, noise factor as a plain ratio, and noise temperature in kelvin. They describe the same thing, and switching between them takes two short formulas. This guide gives the formulas, a reference table, and a few rules of thumb.

The three quantities

  • Noise factor (F) is a linear ratio: the input signal-to-noise ratio divided by the output signal-to-noise ratio. A perfect device has F = 1.
  • Noise figure (NF) is the noise factor in decibels. A perfect device has NF = 0 dB.
  • Noise temperature (Te) is the temperature of a resistor at the input that would produce the same added noise. A perfect device has Te = 0 K.

The formulas

F = 10NF/10     NF = 10 · log10(F)
Te = T0 · (F − 1)     F = 1 + Te / T0

T0 is the standard reference temperature, 290 K. The same T0 is used by this site's calculator.

Worked examples

  • The 3.4 GHz amplifier from the measurement guide has NF = 2.84 dB. That is F = 100.284 ≈ 1.92, or Te = 290 × 0.92 ≈ 267 K.
  • A datasheet that says Te = 50 K corresponds to F = 1 + 50/290 ≈ 1.172, or NF ≈ 0.69 dB.

Rules of thumb

  • Noise temperature is the better scale when comparing very low-noise devices. Going from NF 0.5 dB to 0.4 dB looks tiny, but it is a drop of about 7 K.
  • A device with NF = 3 dB has a noise temperature roughly equal to the 290 K reference. Each time you add 3 dB, the noise factor roughly doubles.
  • Never average noise figures in dB without thinking. To combine stages, convert to linear noise factors and use the cascade formula, as in the second-stage correction guide.
