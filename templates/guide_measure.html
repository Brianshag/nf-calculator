You don't need a dedicated noise figure analyzer to characterize an LNA. A spectrum analyzer and a calibrated noise source are enough for a measurement good to a few tenths of a dB, as long as the setup is clean and you correct for the analyzer's own noise. This guide walks through a real measurement of an amplifier at 3.4 GHz, from instrument settings to the final number.

What you need

  • A spectrum analyzer with markers and a zero-span mode
  • A noise source with a calibrated ENR (here: 15.13 dB at 3.4 GHz) and its DC supply, switched on and off with a pulse or switch
  • The device under test (DUT), here an amplifier with roughly 10 dB of gain
  • Good coaxial cables and adapters, kept as short as practical

Step 1: Set up the analyzer

The trick that makes this measurement fast is capturing both noise source states in a single sweep. Put the analyzer in zero span at your measurement frequency, set the sweep time to 10 seconds, and toggle the noise source part way through the sweep. In this example the source is OFF at the 5 s marker and ON at the 8 s marker. Settings used here:

Centre frequency3.4 GHz
Span0 Hz (zero span)
RBW / VBW3 MHz / 3 MHz
Sweep time10 s
Scale / reference level2 dB/div, −85 dBm

A wide RBW gives a steadier reading of noise, but only if the DUT response is flat across that bandwidth. Use an average or RMS detector, not peak detection, which reads noise several dB high.

Step 2: Calibration (noise source only)

Connect the noise source straight to the analyzer, with no DUT. Run the sweep and place one marker on the OFF segment and one on the ON segment.

The difference is 4.97 dB, the Y-factor of the analyzer alone. Because the analyzer is a fairly noisy receiver, a 15 dB ENR source only raises its noise floor by about 5 dB, which tells you the analyzer's noise figure is high. That is expected and is exactly what the correction in step 4 removes.

Step 3: Measure with the DUT

Insert the DUT between the noise source and the analyzer without changing any analyzer setting. Repeat the sweep with the same marker times.

The Y-factor is now 10.36 dB, much larger than before because the DUT amplifies the excess noise above the analyzer's own floor. A bigger Y-factor means a more reliable result.

Step 4: Calculate

MeasurementOFF (dBm)ON (dBm)Y-factor
Source only−101.91−96.944.97 dB
Source + DUT−98.42−88.0610.36 dB
  1. With ENR = 15.13 dB, the source-only Y-factor gives an analyzer noise figure of about 11.82 dB.
  2. The DUT + analyzer Y-factor gives a combined (cascaded) noise figure of about 5.19 dB.
  3. The DUT gain, from the difference between ON and OFF powers with and without the DUT, is about 10.13 dB.
  4. Removing the analyzer's contribution with the Friis equation leaves a DUT noise figure of about 2.84 dB.

Result: the amplifier has a noise figure of about 2.84 dB and a gain of about 10.1 dB at 3.4 GHz. Skipping the correction would have reported 5.19 dB.

You can reproduce every number above by typing the four readings and the ENR into the calculator. See why the second-stage correction matters for the derivation, and common Y-factor mistakes before you trust a number you will publish.
